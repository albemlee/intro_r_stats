# Inferential Statistics

## The limitation of descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics describes your sample. But in research, we care about the *population* — the broader group our sample came from.

The challenge: we can never measure the entire population. We can only work with our sample and use it to make *inferences* about the population.

> **Inferential statistics** uses sample data to draw conclusions about a population or underlying mechanism — while honestly accounting for uncertainty.

---

## The penguin example revisited

Suppose our sample shows Chinstrap penguins have longer bills than Adelie penguins. But:

- Our sample has only 344 penguins
- A different random sample might give a slightly different result
- How confident are we that the difference is real — not just sampling variation?

This is exactly the question inferential statistics answers.

---

## The three tools of inferential statistics

| Tool | What it tells you |
|------|------------------|
| **Point estimate** | Your best single guess at the population value |
| **Standard error** | How much your estimate might vary across samples |
| **Confidence interval** | A range that likely contains the true population value |

Together, these replace a single number (your sample statistic) with an honest statement about what the population probably looks like.

---

## Knowledge check

A study measures average systolic blood pressure in 500 patients and finds a mean of 128 mmHg. Which of the following is an inferential statement?

a) "The mean systolic BP in our sample is 128 mmHg."  
b) "The average systolic BP in the population is approximately 128 mmHg."

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**b) is inferential.** It makes a claim about the population. Statement a) is purely descriptive — it only describes the sample.

</details>
