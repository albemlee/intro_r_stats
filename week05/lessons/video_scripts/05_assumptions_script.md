## Introduction

Now that you understand the logic of hypothesis testing, the next question is: which test do you use? The answer depends on your data and, critically, on whether your data meets the assumptions of the test you're considering.

---

## [SLIDE: You Don't Need to Memorize Every Statistical Test]

Here's a reassuring parallel to what we said about functions and visualizations: you don't need to memorize every statistical test. There are dozens of them.

But unlike functions and visualizations, you do need to understand the **assumptions** behind the tests you use. Applying a test to data that violates its assumptions can produce completely unreliable results — p-values that are too small or too large, confidence intervals that don't cover what they should.

---

## [SLIDE: Parametric vs. Non-Parametric Tests]

The most important distinction in test selection is parametric versus non-parametric.

**Parametric tests** assume your data was generated by a specific distribution — usually the normal distribution. Because they use this information, they're generally more powerful: better at detecting real effects when they exist.

**Non-parametric tests** make no distributional assumption. They're more flexible but typically less powerful than parametric tests when the normality assumption holds.

If your data is normally distributed, use a parametric test. If not, use a non-parametric alternative.

---

## [SLIDE: Checking the Normality Assumption]

Before choosing a test, you need to assess whether your data is normally distributed. Use three tools:

**1. Histogram** — a bell-shaped, symmetric distribution suggests normality. Any obvious skew or multimodality is a warning sign.

**2. QQ plot** — plots your data quantiles against the theoretical quantiles of a normal distribution. Points falling along the diagonal line suggest normality; systematic deviations indicate departures.

```r
gentoo_flipper <- penguins %>%
  filter(species == "Gentoo") %>%
  pull(flipper_length_mm)

qqnorm(gentoo_flipper)
qqline(gentoo_flipper)
```

**3. Shapiro-Wilk test** — a formal test of normality:

```r
shapiro.test(gentoo_flipper)
```

- p > 0.05: fail to reject normality → parametric test is appropriate
- p ≤ 0.05: evidence against normality → use a non-parametric test

Use all three together. The Shapiro-Wilk test has low power with small samples (may miss non-normality) and very high power with large samples (may flag minor, inconsequential departures). The visual checks provide important context.

## Test Selection in Practice

Here's a quick guide to the tests covered in this course:

| Question | Data type | Normal? | Test |
|----------|-----------|---------|------|
| Compare two groups | Continuous | Yes | t-test |
| Compare two groups | Continuous | No | Wilcoxon rank-sum |
| Compare 3+ groups | Continuous | Yes | One-way ANOVA |
| Association: 2 categorical variables | Categorical | — | Chi-squared |

## Knowledge Check

You want to compare hospital stay duration between patients in treatment group A versus treatment group B. A histogram shows strong right skew. Which test should you use?

**Wilcoxon rank-sum test** — a non-parametric alternative to the t-test. The data is not normally distributed, so the t-test's assumption is violated.
