# Descriptive Statistics

## What descriptive statistics does — and doesn't do

**Descriptive statistics** summarizes and describes your sample. That is all it does. It does not allow you to draw conclusions about the broader population.

This distinction matters enormously:

| Statement | Valid? |
|-----------|--------|
| "In our sample of 344 penguins, Chinstrap penguins have longer bills on average than Adelie penguins." | ✅ Descriptive — describes the sample |
| "Chinstrap penguins have longer bills than Adelie penguins." | ❌ Inferential claim — requires inferential statistics |

Always be precise about what your data can and cannot tell you.

---

## Choosing the right summary

The right descriptive statistics depend on the **type of variable**:

| Variable type | Visualization | Summary statistics |
|--------------|--------------|-------------------|
| Categorical | Bar plot | Counts, proportions |
| Continuous (normal) | Histogram | Mean, standard deviation |
| Continuous (skewed) | Histogram | Five-number summary (min, Q1, median, Q3, max) |

> **Rule:** Always plot your data first. The shape of the distribution determines which summary statistics are appropriate.

## Knowledge check

A researcher analyzes 200 hospital patients and finds 60% have hypertension. Write:
1. One **accurate** descriptive conclusion
2. One **inaccurate** conclusion that oversteps the data

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

1. ✅ "In our sample of 200 hospital patients, 60% have a hypertension diagnosis."
2. ❌ "Most hospital patients have hypertension." — This generalizes from the sample to a broader population without inferential justification.

</details>
